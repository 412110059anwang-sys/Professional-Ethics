## Part 1: Moral Theories: An Overview

### Slide 2: Priority in Moral Decision Making
When making a moral decision, what is your priority:
- A) Are you concerned with rules?
- B) Are you concerned with consequences?
- C) Are you concerned with character development?
- D) Are you concerned with duties?
- E) Are you concerned with virtues and the virtuous life?
- F) Are you concerned with obeying authority or religious traditions?
- G) Are you concerned about demonstrating care and empathy?

### Slide 3-4: Definition of a Moral Theory
- A moral theory is a perspective on moral situations that we adopt or that we value.
- It is the way one chooses to live out and to interpret one's values.
- It is also the way a professional puts into practice the choices made about what is valuable in his or her profession or occupation.
- A moral theory involves a set of assumptions or claims about how moral situations ought to be addressed, and which component of a moral problem should take priority.
- For example, to solve a moral problem, are consequences more important than duties, are intentions more important than preserving caring relationships between the conflicting moral agents?

### Slide 5-6: A) Moral Relativism
- The most extreme version of relativism is called **moral subjectivism**.
- An action is considered to be valid and acceptable based solely on the beliefs and the preferences of the individual.
- **Cultural relativism** maintains that acceptable and valid forms of moral behaviors are based on the norms established by the larger social group in which one resides.
- **The problems with subjectivism**:
  - A) One is expected to tolerate all forms of moral behavior, regardless of motives and outcomes.
  - B) There are to be no evaluations of moral behavior based on normative standards.
  - C) There are no grounds upon which to criticize a culture's practices. Each culture's views of what is appropriate and good are as good as any others.
  - D) There are no standards for such a critique beyond that we just do not like it.

### Slide 7: B) Moral Absolutism
- At the other end of the spectrum of moral theories are the absolutist approaches.
- These approaches maintain that certain moral conduct or behaviors must apply to all persons at all times (i.e. keeping promises or preserving human life).
- Various religious traditions adhere to the absolutist views, placing unqualified prohibitions on everything from the intentional killing of innocent humans, to divorce, to suicide.
- One must know the rules. One need only know the rules and make sure that one follow these rules categorically.

### Slide 8: The Right and The Good
- Nearly all moral theories are concerned with **the right** and **the good**.
- **The right** typically refers to the means by which moral agents make their choices, whereas **the good** refers to the outcome that is being sought or promoted.
- Deontological theories focus on the right, and consequentialism refers to outcomes or consequences.

### Slide 9-12: C) Deontologists & Immanuel Kant
- The motives behind moral behavior are important considerations, and good motives are essential to acting rightly.
- So long as one is choosing and acting for the right reasons, one is being moral, even if bad results materialize.
- The key to acting rightly is acting upon principles, and one has a duty to abide by them.
- Deontologists are therefore more concerned with motives than with outcomes.
- **Immanuel Kant**:
  - a. It is always wrong to lie.
  - b. It is always wrong to break promises.
  - c. One cannot allow his or her emotions to alter one's duty, whatever that may be.
  - Accountability: The moral agent must be held accountable/responsible for her moral behavior and choices.
  - **Universalizability**: Standards of evaluation that apply equally to all persons at all times and in all circumstances.
  - **Reason alone**: Moral choices should be based on reason alone; no subjective considerations can influence our choices.
  - **Impartiality**: Seek at least a reasonable standard of impartiality in our choices.
  - **Motive counts**: Are you acting to benefit another person, or simply yourself?
  - **Dignity & Categorical Imperative**: Human persons are special; they are holders of dignity and are therefore worthy of profound moral respect. "Always treat persons, oneself included, as ends in themselves and never as mere means." This is called a **categorical imperative**.

### Slide 13-16: D) Utilitarianism (or Consequentialism)
- Consequentialists focus on acting morally to promote desirable outcomes.
- Human beings wish to avoid physical and psychological pain and to seek pleasure.
- One's moral duty is to strive to behave in such a way to produce the most good and the most desirable consequences, those that produce pleasure and not pain.
- **Ends justify the means**: The ends, if they are right ones and of sufficient worth, will always justify any means taken to achieve them.
- **Potential sacrifice of the innocent**: Producing the best outcomes for as many persons involved (the most pleasure over pain) sometimes may result in the sacrifice of an innocent individual.
- **Utility & Equal Treatment**: Acting justly aims to produce the most good for the highest number of people.
- **Example**: Individuals allowing themselves to be tested with experimental vaccines to determine the best vaccine to prevent the spread of Corona Virus 19.
- **Animal Welfare**: Since proper moral behavior is enhancement of pleasure and reduction of pain, humans are not the only beings to whom we owe moral consideration. Most animals, and certainly all mammals, have capacities for experiencing pleasure and pain, so we have a duty to include their welfare in moral decision making.
- **Context & Moral Risk**: Context is important (specific facts and variables of the case). Because humans are unable to predict all morally relevant consequences, there is uncertainty. All practical choices therefore involve a **"moral risk."**

### Slide 17-19: E) W. D. Ross's Theory of Moral Decision Making (Duty Theorist)
- Argues that moral decision making has to take consequences into consideration, since it is important to make moral decisions that cause no harm (**non-maleficence**) and result in good consequences (**beneficence**).
- Self-evident, binding duties with inherent moral force:
  - A. We should be honest.
  - B. We should keep our promises.
  - C. We should help others.
  - D. We should be just in giving people what they deserve and in distributing scarce resources.
  - E. We should be grateful.
  - F. We should act and work to repair harms that we cause.
  - G. We should also strive to make ourselves better human beings.
- **Messy Reality & Moral Risk**: Moral decision making is a messy business; dilemmas are prevalent in daily life; we must pay attention to both past actions and future impacts; persons of good character embrace these challenges, do the best they can, and accept the unavoidable "moral risk."

### Slide 20-21: F) An Ethics of Care (Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings)
- Focuses on persons and relationships.
- There is no right or wrong independent of persons, situations, and relationships.
- An action is right if it expresses care for another or is done to maintain or further a caring relationship.
- **Criticisms**:
  - 1. Does not explain how to move from caring to right action.
  - 2. Cannot be universally applied since definitions of care vary from person to person and culture to culture.

## Part 2: Making Moral Decisions: A Model

### Step I: Determine the Facts
- Accurately and thoroughly determine the morally relevant facts of the situation.

### Step II: Determine the Type of Conflict
- **A. Moral Dilemma**: A conflict in which any choice will violate one or more moral principles, cause harm, and make it hard to be virtuous.
- **B. Moral Ignorance**: You do not know where and how to evaluate options to resolve a moral conflict. Response: Seek wisdom of others with more experience and/or wide-ranging advice from varied backgrounds.
- **C. Moral Distress**: One knows the best (or better) answer but is prevented from acting upon it because of power structures, the law, or economics.

### Step III: Determine Stakeholders and Relationships
- Determine who will be impacted by potential choices (who is at stake and how deeply they will be affected).
- Consider relationships between the various players and types of relationships between professionals.

### Step IV: Determine General and Role-Specific Principles at Stake
- **A. General Principles**: Recognized by all mature persons as binding.
- **B. Role-Specific Principles**: Motivated by particular professional obligations (e.g., a profession's code of ethics).
- **10 Core Moral Principles**:
  - **A. Dignity**: Persons should not be used as mere tools or objects; respect as autonomous agents.
  - **B. Non-maleficence**: Wrong to cause harm for no moral reason.
  - **C. Fidelity**: Keep promises (explicit or implied).
  - **D. Reparation**: Repair harms caused to others (directly/indirectly, intentionally, through gross negligence, or ignorance/carelessness).
  - **E. Honesty**: Avoid deceiving others (overtly or covertly).
  - **F. Beneficence**: Do what you reasonably can to improve the situation and lives of others under your care/guidance.
  - **G. Distributive Justice**: How the community allocates social goods (power, wealth, education; e.g., affirmative action, quota system).
  - **H. Formal Justice**: Give persons what they have legitimately earned and apply social structures (laws, civil rights) in an unbiased manner based only on relevant factors.
  - **I. Gratitude**: Show appreciation to others' actions intended for your benefit.
  - **J. Self-improvement**: Develop moral character to facilitate moral sensitivity, discernment, and resolve.

### Step V: Workplace Evaluation Questions
1. Have you considered all possible moral solutions with the goal of seeking the best, most creative solution?
2. How will the moral choice impact the character of the decision maker and the recipient?
3. How effectively can the proposed solutions be implemented?
4. Which decision will likely produce an environment in which persons, individually and communally, can flourish?

### Step VI: Emergent Obligations
- Once the best available choice is made, determine what obligations, if any, emerge.

### Step VII: Implementation and Transparency
- Implement your choice properly.
- **Commitment to Transparency**: Are you willing to share the decision you made and how you reached it with whoever may ask (superior, manager, chair, director, boss, colleagues)?
